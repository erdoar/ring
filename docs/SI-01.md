# SI-01 — Reverse impulse response vocal isolation

**for ultra-low-latency digital-console inserts**

Field note, October 2026. Not a product manual.

A single close microphone, in series on a console insert, asked to give back the voice and to refuse the room, the PA, the stage, and the beginning of a howl. This paper compares the ways to do that, says which claims survive contact with a USB port, and specifies an architecture that can be built without pretending a laptop is a dedicated touring processor.

| | |
|---|---|
| Question | Plugin or host, and which math, on a console insert |
| Constraint | Monitor paths that notice a handful of milliseconds |
| Not in scope | Stem separation of a finished stereo mix |

Commercial plug-ins are cited from public reviews, vendor pages, and interviews. Nothing here is their source, their weights, or a suggestion to copy a product.

## 1. Verdict

Build a plugin. Do not build a mixer, a driver, or a time-reversed convolution.

The operation that belongs on the insert is a **causal residual subtractor**: from the microphone signal, subtract a late-tail prediction and any narrow resonance that is behaving like feedback rather than like a voice. If a network is used, it estimates that residual from past samples only. It does not store this room's impulse response, and it does not play an impulse response backwards.

Default algorithmic latency is zero samples beyond the host block. Offer a second mode with a fixed, declared delay — 127 or 256 samples at 48 kHz — for front-of-house groups and for broadcast. Never put the delayed mode on a split where a dry copy of the same vocal is still in the mix. A millisecond of disagreement is a comb filter, not depth.

Ultra-low-latency USB is a property of a particular console driver, not of USB as a species. On an Allen & Heath SQ the built-in USB insert measured about 18 ms and barely moved when the buffer changed, which means the desk, not the DAW, owns the number. On a Behringer Wing the same class of test landed near 7 ms. Hardware Dante and MADI on that SQ bench were 2–5 ms. A singer on in-ears can hear the difference, and some singers can hear less than that. The first screen of the product is a loopback measurement of *this* insert. The strength knob comes second.

Honest performance, if the work is done well, is on the order of 6–12 dB more usable gain on a difficult lav or headset, and a drier voice, without a new notch in the tone. It is not 40 dB. Forty decibels is what a dedicated touring processor currently advertises, on MADI, under 8.5 ms, inside one manufacturer's system. That number is a ceiling to respect, not a target to print on a USB plugin.

## 2. Pros and cons, before the design

Nine families are in market or in the literature. They are not interchangeable, and the marketing names overlap. The failure column is what the engineer hears at soundcheck.

### Ring-out EQ

Static graphic or parametric notches. Latency: zero, if it is the console EQ. Fits a known room, one talker position, a patient engineer.

Holds up:

- Sample-accurate, crash-proof, already on the desk.
- A few narrow, deep cuts at real modes are inaudible and stable.
- Does not depend on a computer in the signal path.

Breaks:

- The howl moves when the singer moves, the set changes, or the graphic is recalled wrong.
- Five or six notches and the vocal goes thin. The tone budget is spent.
- Does nothing about PA spill, crowd, or the late tail. Those still eat gain before feedback.

### Automatic notch

Howling detector plus a stack of narrow cuts. Latency: near zero. Detection takes a few periods of the tone. Fits an emergency backup, speech systems, installed rooms.

Holds up:

- Cheap, robust, and fast enough that a mic pointed at a wedge does not take the show down.
- A well-estimated frequency (tenths of a hertz) can be cut so narrowly that speech barely notices.
- No model file, no driver, no scene that forgets a license.

Breaks:

- It removes signal, not a path. A sung note that sits on the howl gets the same hole.
- Academic adaptive-notch analyses show a hard ceiling: push much past a few extra dB of stable gain and the howl count explodes faster than the notches can settle. One teaching case stops converging around +5 dB over the uncontrolled maximum stable gain. That is not a product spec, but it is why "just add more notches" fails.
- Reverb and bleed are still in the mic. The fader still comes up into a washy vocal.

### Adaptive feedback cancellation

Estimate the speaker-to-mic path and subtract it. Latency: can be one sample, plus whatever decorrelation is inserted. Fits hearing aids, installed speech, a single known loudspeaker feed.

Holds up:

- In principle it removes the feedback component and leaves the voice, which is the right goal.
- Tracks a slowly moving path better than a frozen notch.
- A long literature (prediction-error method, probe noise, band-limited adaptation). It works in hearing aids because the loop is short and the loudspeaker signal is fully known.

Breaks:

- The voice and the feedback are the same voice. An unconstrained adaptive filter will cancel the singer. Adaptation must freeze on voiced frames, or the loop must be decorrelated (frequency shift, noise, delay, nonlinearity). Decorrelation is audible if overdone.
- A live console does not hand you one loudspeaker reference. FOH, wedges, fills, and a choir of mics are many paths.
- Early reflections you might have wanted are part of the path. A deep cancel dries the vocal and can sound gated.

### Classical inverse filter

Deconvolve the room impulse response. The good approximate inverse wants a modeling delay. A zero-delay inverse is a worse inverse. Fits offline restoration, a fixed mic, a fixed loudspeaker, a lab.

Holds up:

- If H is known, minimum-phase, and time-invariant, 1/H recovers the source.
- Least-squares FIR inverses are well understood. Regularization keeps them from exploding between the zeros.

Breaks:

- A room IR is mixed-phase. The maximum-phase zeros do not have a stable causal inverse. You either look ahead or you live with an approximation.
- Move the mic a few centimetres and the deep null collapses. At 4 kHz the wavelength is about 8 cm. Spatial coherence, not CPU, is the limit.
- You do not want to invert the capsule, the proximity effect, or the singer. A full inverse treats them as distortion.

### Time-reversed IR

Convolve with the flipped impulse response. Latency equals the length of the IR: tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Fatal on a monitor. Fits nothing on a live vocal insert. Useful as a matched filter in radar, not here.

Holds up:

- Time reversal focuses energy for a known path in a reciprocal medium.
- As a matched filter it maximises correlation against that exact IR.

Breaks:

- It is not an inverse. Convolution with h[-n] does not undo convolution with h[n] unless h is a very special signal.
- It smears the voice by the length of the room and adds that length as delay.
- Any product that says "reverse IR" and means this operation is solving the wrong equation. In the live plugins that actually work, the phrase means the learned kernel below.

### Weighted prediction error

Predict the late tail from older frames and subtract it. Latency is the prediction delay, often one STFT frame. A short block can hold this under a few milliseconds. Fits dereverb of a single voice when the delay is one host block, or a little more.

Holds up:

- It does not need a measured IR. The tail is predictable from the recent past because late reverberation is dense and slowly varying.
- A delay before the prediction protects the direct sound, so the voice is not cancelled with the tail.
- Online and time-varying versions exist. This is the right classical parent of a live dereverb.

Breaks:

- A long STFT window, which is how most papers get their scores, is incompatible with in-ears. The algorithm has to be rewritten at block rate.
- It is a dereverb, not a feedback canceller. A growing howl is a pole outside the model it was derived for.
- Multichannel WPE wants several mics of the same voice. A console insert is one channel.

### Learned cancellation kernel

Estimate, in real time, a short filter that nulls residue. Latency: zero if the kernel is applied with coefficients from the past only. Quality jumps if you allow 2–6 ms. This is the thing worth building for a vocal insert.

Holds up:

- Matches the public description of real-time residue cancellation: train on many real rooms plus synthetic howls, and at runtime form the kernel that would cancel the non-voice residue.
- One insert, one voice, no threshold for the engineer to miss. Public tests describe on the order of 10 dB of steady residue reduction at a moderate setting, and a reverb-return loop that stopped regenerating when the process was engaged.
- Field reports are consistent in kind if not in number: more gain before feedback on lavs and headsets, less room, less bleed. One lav-in-front-of-PA demo is the loud version (on the order of 10 dB). A working-engineer note is a more modest 6–8 dB. Another public claim is at least about 6 dB. Treat the spread as the spread of rooms, not as one spec.

Breaks:

- A kernel fit to the last 50 ms is wrong for the next 50 ms if the singer walks or a second howl appears. Cap the subtraction or the miss becomes a swoosh.
- It will eat sounds that are not voice. A tambourine living in the vocal mic can disappear. That is the algorithm working. It is also a musical failure if the tambourine was meant to stay.
- Public writeups say the plugin itself adds no latency. The USB or analog insert around it still does. Reviewers have had to time-align the path by hand.
- Training-data bias: churches and corporate rooms dominate the origin stories. A loud drum kit two metres from an omni headset is a different distribution. Publish the failure set.

### Causal neural mask

Per-band keep-gain, no lookahead, or a short fixed lookahead. Latency: zero reported plugin delay, or a fixed 127 samples in the designs that admit a quality mode. Fits the monitor path, and front of house if you can spend about 3 ms.

Holds up:

- A public self-contained VST3/AU exists with ZERO mode at 0.0 ms plugin delay and a LOW mode of 127 samples (2.65 ms at 48 kHz), aimed at live plugin hosts.
- A mask is zero-phase inside the block. You are not designing a mixed-phase inverse. You are deciding, per band, how much of this block is voice.
- Sustained notes, vibrato, and falsetto can be kept if the network was trained on singing and not only on speech.

Breaks:

- At a 32-sample block the spectrum is coarse. A howl and a harmonic a few hertz apart share a bin. The mask alone will notch the note or pass the howl.
- Heavy compression before the insert lifts the residue into the voice. Dynamics belong after isolation.
- Zero algorithmic latency is not zero path latency. The claim is about plugin delay compensation. The console still owes the round trip.

### Dedicated source separation

A large model on a purpose-built processor. Latency: under 8.5 ms end-to-end over MADI, per the vendor. Fits a tour that already owns the processor. Not a USB insert.

Holds up:

- The 2026 touring benchmark: up to about 40 dB of non-voice reduction, four instances, MADI / Milan-AVB / AES, redundant power and network, accepted by touring engineers at front of house.
- It removes crowd, PA spill, instrument bleed, and the ring that becomes feedback, before the fader.
- It shows the ceiling if you may spend a dedicated DSP and a single-digit millisecond budget.

Breaks:

- It is not a plugin and it is not USB. It wants a specific processor and at least three of that maker's amplified controllers. Other spatial products on the same processor cannot run beside it.
- 8.5 ms is a front-of-house number. It is the wrong default for a singer's ears.
- You cannot ship this class of reduction, at this latency, on a spare laptop hanging off an SQ USB port.

### What the comparison forces

- Notches are the safety net, not the product. They are audible once they stack, and they do not dry a room.
- Full-path adaptive feedback cancellation wants the loudspeaker reference. An insert on the mic channel usually does not have it. Steal the freeze-on-voice idea. Do not reimplement a hearing aid.
- A literal inverse, and a time-reversed IR, both fail the latency test or the stability test or both.
- Weighted prediction error is the right dereverb skeleton. A short resonator bank is the right howl skeleton. A causal mask or a learned kernel is how you decide the gains without a threshold the engineer has to ride.
- Dedicated separation proves the audio result and prices you out of it. Copy the problem statement. Do not copy the latency budget or the decibel claim.

## 3. Three filters people call "reverse IR"

The phrase is doing too much work. Three operations get the same name, and only one belongs in this product.

**Inverse.** `Ŝ(z) = Y(z) / H(z)`. Undo the room. Right equation, usually unstable or non-causal for a real H, and it also undoes the microphone.

**Matched filter.** `y[n] ∗ h[−n]`. Time-reversed convolution. It searches for the path. It does not invert it, and it delays by the length of h.

**Cancellation kernel.** `e[n] = y[n] − (g ∗ y)[n]`. Subtract an estimate of everything in y that is not the voice. g is short, causal, and recomputed from the past. This is the operation the working live tools are pointing at when they say reverse IR.

The public account of that class of plugin is the third kind. The training loop convolves voice with a library of measured room responses, nulls the voice, and keeps the residue. The network then learns the filter which, applied to the mixture, cancels that residue. A separate simulator throws growing feedback tones into the same pool so the dereverb also treats howl as a kind of residue. A real-time version of this, as opposed to an offline ambience-removal render, is the part that is new. In published listening tests the plugin itself added no latency.

The kernel is only as good as the residue it was taught. Feedback that has not yet grown, a second talker, a harmonica, a deliberately roomy vocal: each of those is a case you either train or you openly fail.

## 4. What the microphone actually contains

Write the insert input as four terms, not one "dirty vocal":

```
y[n] = s[n] + (h ∗ s)[n] + b[n] + v[n]
```

`s` is the voice you mean to keep, including capsule tone and proximity. `h` is only the late tail relative to a protected horizon. `b` is bleed and PA return. `v` is hiss, HVAC, handling.

Feedback is not a fifth mystery. It is `b` routed through the PA and back into the capsule, with loop gain near or above unity, so a narrow band grows. Early in that growth it is a high-Q resonance. Late, it is a sine wave that owns the channel. A process that waits for the sine wave has already lost the show. A process that swings at every resonance will notch vibrato.

Consequences:

- Do not invert `s`. Body resonance, proximity, and the consonant are the vocal. Protect a horizon of one host block at minimum, and never adapt the tail filter hard during a clearly voiced frame.
- `h` is long and smooth. It can be predicted from older samples. That prediction does not need lookahead. This is why dereverb can be causal even though a true inverse cannot.
- `b` from a drum kit is neither a tail nor a tone. A one-channel insert can only duck it by deciding it is not voice. That decision will be wrong on beatboxing, megaphones, and anyone playing a shaker into their own mic. Those cases go in the manual.
- Spatial coherence kills measured-IR tricks. A fixed inverse measured at soundcheck is a different filter by the second verse. Online estimation is the only version that survives a human being on stage.

## 5. Latency, and why USB is the whole argument

"Zero latency" on a plugin means the plugin adds no samples beyond the block the host already took. One 2026 vocal plugin states this cleanly: ZERO mode at 0.0 ms, and a LOW mode of 127 samples reported as plugin delay compensation. A touring processor is equally clean from the other direction: processing takes time, zero does not exist, and their end-to-end figure over MADI is under 8.5 ms, which their engineers accepted at front of house.

Lester and Boley, AES 123, 2007, paper 7198, are still the right citation for monitors, and they refuse a single threshold. Depending on instrument and on wedge versus in-ear, the delay people objected to ran from 42 ms down to possibly under 1.4 ms. In-ears were stricter than wedges. Vocalists notice. A 2012 DAGA study (paper 000239) found that putting a little room back into the ear mix made delays up toward the low teens more acceptable. That cuts both ways: a strong dereverb on the ear path removes the air that was hiding a late insert. Run less dereverb in the ears than at front of house, or the singer will call the plugin "digital" when the real problem is a dried, delayed path.

Published insert numbers worth designing against, at idle or near-idle hosts. Plugin delay is **not** included. The host buffer **is** included. Do not add it twice.

"Focused on ultra-low-latency USB" therefore means: target the consoles whose USB device can hold a 32- or 64-sample stream with an empty-insert round trip under 8 ms, publish the list from measurement, and refuse to imply that every class-compliant multitrack port is in the list.

Engineering policy, not a law of hearing. Total = path + plugin delay. Inside / marginal / outside:

| Use | Inside | Marginal beyond that, until |
|---|---|---|
| Vocal in-ears | ≤ 5 ms | 8 ms |
| Wedge vocal | ≤ 8 ms | 14 ms |
| FOH music, the vocal group delayed as one | ≤ 12 ms | 20 ms |

Distance equivalent: milliseconds times 0.343 metres. 7.3 ms is about 2.5 m of extra path. 18 ms is about 6.2 m.

Clocking is part of latency. The console is master. A second free-running interface in the same insert drifts, then clicks, then gets blamed on the algorithm. 48 kHz is the live rate that matters. 96 kHz halves the sample period and doubles the CPU, and many console USB ports will not do it. At 48 kHz a 32-sample block is 0.67 ms. The processing for one voice has to finish inside that window with margin, on the computer that will actually be at the gig, with the other plugins loaded. A separation model that sometimes takes 4 ms is not "usually fine." It is a tick in the PA.

## 6. Architecture that fits the budget

One instance per voice. Sample rate follows the console. The host block is 32 or 64 samples. The plugin reports its own extra delay and nothing else. Bypass is bit-transparent and ramps inside a block so a scene change does not click.

1. **Protect.** The most recent block is never the input to the tail predictor. Call that horizon D. At ZERO, D is one block. The direct sound, the consonant, and the mic tone live here and are passed.
2. **Predict the tail.** A short causal filter, a block-rate child of weighted prediction error, estimates late energy from samples older than D. Adapt it slowly, and freeze or shrink the step size when the frame is strongly voiced. Subtract, then cap the subtraction. A miss may dull the room. It may not duck the syllable.
3. **Track resonators separately.** Howl is not late reverberation. Follow a handful of narrow partials. A partial may be turned down only when it is both persistent and high-Q and the voice-likelihood at that frequency is low. Vibrato, formant motion, and a syllabic envelope are evidence of voice. A pure tone that grows while the rest of the spectrum does not is evidence of feedback. When those disagree, keep the voice. A howl parked on a sung note is the published failure, and it is the correct failure.
4. **Voice likelihood, causal only.** Pitch from the previous block, harmonic-stack consistency, 4–8 Hz modulation, a small network over the last few blocks of bands if you must. No future frame. The network's job is the gain of the subtractors, not a second copy of the waveform. If the network is late, the show still has audio.
5. **Declare delay, or don't.** ZERO does steps 1–4 inside the host block and reports 0 samples. LOW adds a fixed 127-sample modeling delay and reports 127. A 256-sample front-of-house mode (5.33 ms at 48 kHz) may use a least-squares approximate inverse with a modeling delay. That is a better inverse than the zero-delay one, and it is still the wrong tool for ears.

Place the insert after the channel EQ that feeds the PA and before the compressor. EQ in front means the isolator sees the loop the loudspeakers actually close. Compression in front lifts residue into the voice. A gate anywhere in this chain is an admission that the isolator is not ready. Summing several talkers to one group and inserting once is right for one talker and wrong for two: a single-voice model will keep the louder voice and damage the other.

Caps, as a starting spec: broadband residual subtraction limited to about 12 dB, a confirmed resonator allowed to go deeper, and a slow release so a departing howl does not uncover as a whoosh. Strength is a scale on those caps, not a threshold. Dereverb is a second scale, on the tail filter only, defaulting lower on any path section 5 marks as marginal for ears.

Four hundred microseconds per voice at 32 samples and 48 kHz is a sane ceiling if other plugins exist. That pays for a few biquads, a short prediction filter, and a very small network. It does not pay for a 2048-point transform with overlap. Resolution in ZERO mode is intentionally poor. That is why howl lives in resonators, where a biquad can be narrow without a long window, and why the tail filter uses history instead of trying to see the future.

## 7. Plugin, or a primary application

**Plugin first.** VST3 and AU. CLAP if a host you care about already speaks it. The console already has the insert, the mute group, the scene, and the preamp. The live hosts already solved sandboxing and snapshots. You inherit their buffer, which is the latency policy. You also inherit their crash. A plugin cannot throw a hardware bypass relay. For theatre and broadcast that eventually matters. It does not matter enough to delay the first version.

**A small meter, not a DAW.** Ship a companion that does one thing: play a blip, time the return, and say whether this USB port is legal for ears, wedges, front of house, or speech. The tables above are the prototype of that screen, using other people's measurements. The real one uses the engineer's.

A full primary application — your own engine, routing, and scene list — means you are now an audio-driver company. Do it only if you need the watchdog relay and the locked buffer, and do it after the plugin has a failure set you believe.

Analog insert through a known interface remains the right wiring diagram on desks whose USB port measures like the SQ. Native insert through Wing-class USB is the right diagram when the meter agrees. MADI or hardware Dante is the right diagram when the channel count grows and the ears are unforgiving. In every case the plugin is the same binary. The path is the variable. Selling "USB zero latency" as a single phrase is how the product gets a reputation for flamming vocals that it did not itself delay.

## 8. How you will know it works

Do not publish a single gain-before-feedback number from a phone video. Publish a distribution on a repeatable loop: a microphone, a loudspeaker, a room or a convolved impulse, loop gain raised until the uncontrolled system howls, then again with the process on. Report the added stable gain as a range across rooms and positions. Expect the difference between a lav in front of a PA and a handheld cardioid on a loud band, and do not sand it down.

Voice damage is the other axis, and speech-codec scores will not measure it. Record a dry close vocal, mix it with bleed and a tail you can also remove, and listen. The panel is singing, vibrato, breath, plosives, a held note a semitone from a howl, and a dead-room take that should come back unchanged. If the treated-room bypass and the engaged plugin do not match on that last take, the likelihood estimator is inventing work.

The failure set ships with the installer:

- Two voices on one instance.
- A sung note parked on the howl frequency.
- Beatbox, harmonica, throat singing, a megaphone.
- Tambourine or guitar in the vocal mic that the artist meant to keep.
- Heavy compression before the insert.
- Audience mics, talkback, and drum overheads. The process is not for them.
- A buffer change or a sample-rate change mid-show. It may glitch once. It may not howl.

There is a level-safety consequence to getting this right. Ten extra decibels of gain before feedback is ten extra decibels the system can deliver when the mask opens on a shout. Put a limiter after the insert. Do not use the isolator as the limiter.

## 9. Build order

0. **This paper.** Decide the path before training a network. If the only available insert is 18 ms, the show is speech-only or the transport has to change. No model fixes an SQ USB port.
1. **Causal DSP, no network.** Tail predictor plus resonator rules plus a hand-built voice likelihood. ZERO mode. Evaluate on recorded lav-and-PA loops. If this cannot hold 6 dB without chewing the vowel, a network will not save the structure.
2. **Learned gains.** Train only the gains of those subtractors. Data: measured rooms, synthetic poles for howl, singing as well as speech, and the adversarial note-on-the-howl. Keep the cap. Keep the freeze.
3. **Plugin and meter.** VST3 and AU. Plugin delay honest. Companion loopback meter. One page in the manual that is section 5, filled in for the desks you have actually timed.
4. **Watchdog, only if the gigs demand it.** A small host with a dry fallback if the callback overruns, plus a mute back to the console. This is the first moment a primary application is justified.

Phase 1 is allowed to sound ordinary. Ordinary and on time is a shippable wedge tool. Extraordinary and late is a demo.
